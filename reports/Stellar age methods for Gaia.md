# Turning discordant stellar clocks into Gaia ages

Useful stellar ages come from three groups of stars. Isochrones date subgiants and turn-off stars, where wide binaries confirm **5–10%** precision when spectroscopic [Fe/H], [α/Fe] and a parallax are in hand. A mass dates red giants, at about **11% statistical** error for APOKASC-3 seismology and 20–40% for spectroscopic mass proxies. Rotation, activity and lithium date young dwarfs from about 10 Myr to 3 Gyr. The old main sequence holds most of Gaia's two billion stars, and it carries almost no age information beyond the prior. Every absolute age traces back to a stellar model or to a cluster ladder dated with isochrones, and that includes gyrochronology, lithium and chemical clocks. The spread between model grids, about **20% or 1–2 Gyr at old ages**, is larger than the best statistical errors, so accuracy, not precision, is the binding constraint. The project's consensus design is therefore the right lever. Per-catalogue maps, learned error inflation, censoring and co-natal equality each address a documented failure of today's catalogues: posteriors dominated by their priors, zero points inherited from training sets, age caps, and leakage of input-parameter scales. No consensus can remove systematics that every isochrone and seismic pipeline shares. Only clocks built on different physics can test the absolute scale: white-dwarf cooling in wide binaries, lithium-depletion boundaries, clusters with eclipsing binaries, globular clusters and actinide chronometry. They number only in the hundreds, so they should referee between the APOKASC-3 and Xiang & Rix gauges rather than replace them. The highest-value additions are a curated benchmark-cluster ladder with free cluster ages, evolved+dwarf and white-dwarf+main-sequence wide pairs, rotation and lithium entered as observables with maps restricted to their valid windows, and the newer seismic and [C/N] samples grouped by the scale they were calibrated on. Gaia DR4 (2 December 2026) will sharpen the metallicities that limit subgiant ages and add 18.2 million rotation-modulated stars for young dwarfs. PLATO and Roman will begin to supply the seismic bridge between dwarfs and giants, which is currently the weakest link.

## Age information lives on three islands, and most of Gaia sits between them

Stellar physics fixes where ages can be measured. A subgiant crosses quickly from core to shell hydrogen burning, so its Teff changes fast while its luminosity tracks mass, and isochrones of different ages stay well separated. That is why **turn-off and subgiant stars give the most precise isochrone ages**, while fits to RGB and main-sequence stars typically return "very flat or ill-defined" posteriors ([SPInS on Gaia DR3 + LAMOST DR8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.15781); [Nataf et al. 2024](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2407.18307)). On the unevolved main sequence the isochrones converge. With survey-quality parameters, **stars with log g ≳ 4.4 receive no age information at all** ([Sahlholdt et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.02829)), and late-K and M dwarf ages in the Gaia–Kepler catalogue are uninformative ([Berger et al. 2020 II](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.14671)). A giant's age is essentially its progenitor's main-sequence lifetime, roughly t ∝ M⁻³, so a fractional mass error becomes a fractional age error three to four times larger. Every giant age method is really a mass method ([Pinsonneault et al. 2025, APOKASC-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00102)). Dwarfs therefore need clocks that do not depend on HR-diagram position at all, such as spin-down, magnetic activity and lithium burning, or an age borrowed from a co-natal companion.

In all three groups, **model systematics exceed statistical precision**. Comparisons between widely used grids give about 5% systematic uncertainty in mass and **about 20% in age** for main-sequence and subgiant stars ([Tayar et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07957)). On the same stars with the same inputs, PARSEC ages come out about 1.5 Gyr younger than BaSTI, and Geneva/SYCLIST ages up to 3–4 Gyr younger at old ages ([Boin et al. 2026, NEST](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09540)). On the RGB, four grids disagree by more than 80% when mass is unknown ([Morales, Tayar & Claytor 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17600v1)). Even with seismic masses, a TESS catalogue of 132,000 giants adds a 10% model term ([Theodoridis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08109)). The best per-star statistical errors sit well inside that floor: a median of 7.5% for the Xiang & Rix subgiants ([Shariat, El-Badry & Bhattacharjee 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08675)), 11.1% for APOKASC-3 giants, and 0.064 dex for an XP-trained giant model on its own test set ([Almannaei et al. 2024, SIDRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09040)). A consensus that quotes only statistical widths will understate total error most in the regimes where it looks best. The error budget needs three terms, reported separately: statistical, calibration (from the per-catalogue maps) and scale (from the anchor).

The table's empty row matters most. For an old FGK or M dwarf, the correct predictive distribution is the age distribution of similar stars in the population. The main danger is records that look like measurements but are priors, and the literature documents this repeatedly. Gaia DR3 FLAME spreads Hyades and Pleiades members over ages that follow its Galactic-population template ([Brandner et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03132)), and its metallicity prior is "completely dominant" ([Banik et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00764)). Uninformative posteriors in the Gaia–Kepler–TESS host catalogue pile up near half of the 30 Gyr grid maximum ([Berger et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.11338v1)). NEST dates young clusters systematically too old because it overestimates main-sequence ages ([Boin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09540)). The project's own audit finds the same failure at scale. Every field pipeline places main-sequence members of clusters younger than 1 Gyr at 2–5 Gyr, **+1.0 to +1.4 dex** too old, while quoting σ of 0.04–0.1 dex ([project audit](../docs/design-20261122-age-consensus.md)). The consensus stays honest for these stars only if such records receive near-zero weight.

## Dwarf clocks run in narrow windows calibrated on a disputed cluster ladder

### Gyrochronology works for groups, inside a narrow age window

Gyrochronology is the most developed dwarf clock, and it works best for groups rather than single stars. Interpolating between open-cluster sequences (gyro-interp) gives statistical age uncertainties for ≈5800 K stars that fall from **±38% at 0.2 Gyr to ±12% at 2 Gyr**. Beyond 2.6 Gyr the method is declared inapplicable because calibration data are sparse ([Bouma, Palumbo & Hillenbrand 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08830)). Applied to 23,813 Kepler FGK stars, it recovers open-cluster ages from 0.04 to 2.5 Gyr and agrees with more than 90% of independent lithium ages ([Bouma et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06246)). ChronoFlow, a normalizing flow trained on about 8,000 rotators in 30 clusters, reaches **0.08 dex for cluster ages but 0.7 dex for individual stars** ([Van-Lane et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12244)). What makes rotation a precise clock is pooling coeval stars, not better single-star modelling.

The calibration rests on roughly ten benchmark clusters whose absolute ages are themselves disputed. The ladder runs α Per (85 Myr), the Pleiades (120 Myr), NGC 3532 (300 Myr), Praesepe and the Hyades (670–700 Myr), NGC 6811 (1 Gyr), NGC 752 (1.3 Gyr), NGC 6819 (2.5 Gyr), Ruprecht 147 (2.7 Gyr) and M67 (4 Gyr). A 2026 study grew the NGC 752 benchmark from 8 to 25 single members with periods. It found that gyro-interp and ChronoFlow overestimate the ages of G and early-K dwarfs by **20–50%**. Published ages for that cluster alone range from 1.18 to 1.75 Gyr, and the authors attribute part of the bias to that spread ([NGC 752 calibration, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10077v1)). Any label inherited from gyrochronology inherits this discrete and uncertain age grid.

Three physical effects narrow the usable window further. Spin-down stalls in K dwarfs: stars of 0.55 M☉ stop spinning down for at least 1.3 Gyr, and lower-mass stars stall longer ([Curtis et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.02272)). Magnetic braking weakens above roughly the solar Rossby number, which Metcalfe et al. say "signals the collapse of the global stellar dynamo". Their evidence includes 94 stars with asteroseismic rotation, although all of it comes from one group ([Metcalfe et al. 2025](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991L..17M)). As a result, gyro ages run about **−13% below asteroseismic ages under 6 Gyr and −47% above it**, and the 2026 HAges compilation trusts them only to about 4 Gyr ([HAges, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12647)). Metallicity adds a systematic that a Gaia-scale model must condition on. At fixed mass above about 0.85 M☉, the median period rises steadily with [Fe/H], and the age–metallicity relation cannot explain this ([Kamai, Somers & Perets 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06604)). Gyro-kinematic ages date groups of stars matched in rotation from their velocity dispersion, and reach about 14 Gyr with roughly 1 Gyr uncertainty ([Lu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14990)). That calibration runs through Galactic dynamics, so it is circular for Milky Way structure studies.

### Period catalogues are large but miss slow, old rotators

The TESS All-Sky Rotation Survey holds periods for **1,046,317 stars within 500 pc**, but its completeness declines above 10–13 days ([Boyle, Bouma & Mann 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05586v2)). Gaia DR3 flagged 474,026 rotational-modulation stars ([Distefano et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05500)). The Kepler "Maunder" catalogue gives conformalized periods for 148,746 stars. In 21.5% of them the signal is bimodal and the longer period is the true one, so single-pass periodograms systematically lock onto short aliases ([Kamai et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06604)). Gaia DR4 will carry **18,237,848** rotational-modulation stars, about 38 times DR3 ([Gaia DR4 contents](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/dr4)). Before DR4, these catalogues cover about 0.1% of Gaia, and their selection runs against old stars. A solar-age G dwarf rotates in about 25 days and old K dwarfs in more than 30. Rotation labels are therefore skewed young unless the selection function is modelled.

### Activity tells young from old but is not a clock

Activity relations are calibrated on the same cluster ladder. Chromospheric R′HK ages reach about 0.2 dex for solar-type stars between the Hyades and the Sun ([Mamajek & Hillenbrand 2008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0807.1686)). LAMOST work disputes the claim that activity flattens after a few Gyr. R′HK tracks age over 2–13 Gyr for G dwarfs but does not decline for F dwarfs ([Ye et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15438)). A linear decline fits best, and **lower-metallicity F, G and K stars are more active** at fixed age ([Han et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17715)).

Only X-ray and UV data reach tens of millions of stars. eRASS1 contains 138,800 coronal sources matched to Gaia ([Freund et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17282)), and simple empirical forms predict L_X to 0.3 dex ([Stassun & Kounkel 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.08607)). GALEX holds about 83 million sources to NUV ≈ 20.8 ([Bianchi et al. 2017](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/1704.05903)). Normalized NUV declines clearly with age for late-F to G dwarfs among 386,500 cluster and field stars ([Li et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18559)). Broad-band UV predicts age with about 0.4 dex scatter ([older UV–age study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1105.1377)). UV excess is also produced by unresolved white-dwarf companions: a 2026 study found 2,597 such WD+FGK candidates within 350 pc ([Gaia–GALEX study, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25216)). Metallicity and dust (R_NUV ≈ 6.8) also modulate it. At Gaia scale, activity can separate young from old stars but cannot measure age.

### Lithium is the young-star clock with the least model dependence

Ages from the lithium depletion boundary (LDB) carry 7–11% systematics ([Jeffries & Naylor 2001](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0009179)) and differ by only about 10% between models ([Jeffries 2014](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/1404.7156)). They apply only to coeval groups aged about 20–200 Myr. For single stars, EAGLES models lithium equivalent width against Teff and age, calibrated on 6,200 stars in 52 Gaia-ESO clusters ([Jeffries et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12197)). It gives better than 10% for young groups, but systematics reach a factor of 2 in older clusters. It is valid only for −0.3 < [Fe/H] < 0.2, and is most sensitive for M dwarfs at 10–100 Myr, K dwarfs at 30–300 Myr and G dwarfs at 0.1–1 Gyr.

GALAH DR4 provides lithium equivalent widths for 892,223 stars, with 417,825 detections and 474,398 upper limits ([GALAH DR4](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19858v2)), and LAMOST MRS adds 455,752 stars ([LAMOST MRS Li](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01815v1)). The hierarchical CHRONOS model recovers 124.5 Myr for the Pleiades, consistent with its LDB age ([CHRONOS I](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.25957)). Lithium upper limits bound age from below, so they belong in a censored likelihood rather than being discarded. The 6708 Å line is far too narrow for XP to resolve, so an XP head can learn lithium ages only through correlated properties.

### Combining clocks raises precision, and can hide a chemistry dependence

stardate combines rotation with isochrones and improves main-sequence ages up to threefold when periods are known to 5% ([Angus et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.07528)). The only dwarf-age method proven at the million-star scale is label transfer through wide binaries: each dwarf inherits the isochrone age of its turn-off or subgiant primary. An XGBoost model trained this way on LAMOST spectra dates about 4 million dwarfs, with **10–25% precision for K dwarfs** ([Wang, Xiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03019)). The paper's own diagnostic matters for this project. Masking Ca II H&K and Hα leaves the ages almost unchanged, because the signal comes mostly from chemical abundances acting as a chemical clock. A data-driven dwarf-age model can therefore quietly learn the Galaxy's age–abundance relation instead of stellar spin-down.

## Turn-off, subgiant and giant ages inherit a 10–20% model floor

### Subgiants are the most precise field clock, if metallicity and α are right

Xiang & Rix fitted about 250,000 LAMOST subgiants to Yonsei–Yale (Y²) isochrones using Gaia parallaxes and spectroscopic [Fe/H] and [α/Fe]. They dated the onset of thick-disc formation to about 13 Gyr ago ([Xiang & Rix 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.12110)). Their update holds about 320,000 subgiants, of which Feltzing et al. used 319,835 ([Feltzing, Feuillet & Bensby 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20959)). It has the lowest dispersion of any published catalogue against NEST/BaSTI emulated ages, with a median σ of 0.79 Gyr ([Boin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09540)).

The only external test of quoted errors for a large field-age catalogue uses wide binaries, measuring σ_z, the scatter of the normalized age difference between components ([Shariat et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08675)). For Xiang & Rix, σ_z = **0.81** over 11 pairs once one outlier with a 3.25σ metallicity mismatch is removed, so the quoted 5–10% errors are realistic. For Nataf's photometric subgiants, σ_z = **2.2–2.7**, meaning their quoted 8–9% errors are two to three times too small. LAMOST giants give σ_z = 1.08, which corresponds to a realistic precision of about 25–30%. Two caveats apply. The positive verdict for Xiang & Rix rests on only 11 pairs. Wide binaries also test only relative errors, so a global scale offset would pass unseen.

Composition dominates the subgiant error budget. Nataf's photometric metallicities run 0.19 ± 0.10 dex higher than Xiang & Rix, with an age ratio of 0.94 ± 0.13 ([Shariat et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08675)). A 0.2 dex error in [α/Fe] shifts subgiant ages by 1–2 Gyr, about the same as switching from BaSTI to PARSEC. Mixing turn-off stars into subgiant samples inflates errors: StarHorse quotes about 30% for the mixed sample and about 15% for subgiants alone ([IAC summary of Queiroz et al. 2023](https://iac.es/en/science-and-technology/publications/starhorse-results-spectroscopic-surveys-and-gaia-dr3-chrono-chemical-populations-solar)).

Priors and caps distort the oldest ages, which are the ones Galactic archaeology needs most. StarHorse unintentionally applied a thin-disc star-formation-history prior that "affect[ed] mainly the oldest ages" ([StarHorse corrigendum](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2023/08/aa45399e-22/aa45399e-22.html)). Sanders & Das ages stop at 13 Gyr, and NEST's MIST grid stops at 12 Gyr ([Boin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09540)). Grids that extend to 20 Gyr place the oldest-star peak at 13.6–13.8 Gyr. Even so, the two studies that do this quote systematic budgets of ±0.4 and ±1.3 Gyr for the same quantity, so no community-standard error floor exists ([Banik et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00764); [Tomasetti et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02692)). A comparison of Gaia DR3 catalogues found ages reliable only below 9–10 Gyr, with older stars underestimated ([INAF DR3 age catalogue](https://openaccess.inaf.it/bitstreams/def49f70-4732-428d-bf22-9d5edba883dd/download)). FLAME will deliver results for about 500 million DR4 sources ([Creevey et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00264)). It is not yet documented whether DR4 drops the solar-abundance assumption that caused FLAME's DR3 failures.

### Giants: mass is the clock, and the mass scale keeps moving

APOKASC-3 covers 15,808 Kepler giants with APOGEE spectra and gives ages for 12,418. Of these, 10,036 reach median uncertainties of **3.8% in mass and 11.1% in age** ([Pinsonneault et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00102)). Its scale is anchored on radii from Gaia luminosities rather than on cluster masses. The seismic mass of NGC 6791 giants (1.15 ± 0.01 M☉) matches the dynamical mass from eclipsing binaries (1.15 ± 0.02 M☉), while NGC 6819 comes out about 2σ above the earlier calibration value. The limits are as clear as the strengths. Replacing GARSTEC with YREC models moves most RGB ages by about ±10%. The catalogue's thick-disc median age, **9.14 ± 0.05 (random) ± 0.9 (systematic) Gyr**, carries a systematic error eighteen times its random one. Luminous α-rich giants disagree with lower-RGB ages by 17–37%, and the scaling relations "break down at the tip of the RGB". An alternative correction scheme cuts the α-rich age discrepancy from −21.8% to −9.6% ([Cao & Pinsonneault 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06609)). Eclipsing binaries confirm that uncorrected scaling relations overestimate red-giant masses: by 12–19% in three systems ([Brogaard et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.08167)) and by 26% for KIC 8430105 ([Thomsen et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05410)).

Metal-poor giants carry biases of opposite sign. Teff-scale errors push seismic masses too high at [M/H] ≤ −1 ([Schonhut-Stasik et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10654)). Global seismic parameters, on the other hand, "severely underestimate" the ages of metal-poor α-rich giants: two Helmi-stream giants come out at 11.2 and 12.5 Gyr once their individual frequencies are modelled ([Lindsay et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01091)). The νmax relation itself appears to depend on metallicity ([Lindsay, Ong & Basu 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03450)).

Red-clump ages depend on how much mass a star lost on the RGB. APOKASC-3 infers **0.10 M☉** (Reimers η = 0.17) with a dispersion of 0.03 M☉, but the metallicity trend is contested. Field giants show mass loss decreasing with increasing [Fe/H] ([Roberts et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10800)), while a metal-poor globular cluster supports mass loss decreasing with decreasing [Fe/H] ([Kalup et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01119)).

About **3–4% of seismic giants are mass-transfer products**, and spectra cannot predict their ages. Examples are 377 of 8,803 APOKASC-3 × MWM stars ([Frazer et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26927)) and 3.33% of field giants compared by [C/N] ([Roberts et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10800)). Cluster tests agree for M67 and NGC 6791 but not for NGC 6819 or M4 ([Tayar & Joyce 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09582)).

Spectroscopic mass proxies extend coverage to where seismology fails. After the first dredge-up, [C/N] gives mass to about 14% and age to about 40% ([Martig et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.08203)). A 2025 calibration on APOKASC-3 recovers seismic ages over 1–10 Gyr with a **mean uncertainty of 1.64 Gyr**. It remains robust for luminous giants, where scaling relations fail ([Roberts et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25321)). A 2026 calibration on 9,122 Kepler giants reaches about 30% over 2–10 Gyr, but is recommended only for lower-RGB stars with [Fe/H] > −1 ([Casali et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21928)). Open-cluster calibrations cover 0.4–6.6 Gyr ([Spoo et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05463)) and have been extended, sparsely, to globular clusters ([Spoo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17579)). Because [C/N] records birth mass and is not reset by mass loss, comparing it with seismic mass flags stars that have lost or gained mass.

### Data-driven transfers copy their parents' zero points

Tayar et al. varied architecture, hyperparameters and training set for 351,995 MWM stars. The ages were "generally insensitive" to the first two but sensitive to the training set, most of all for the oldest, coolest and most metal-poor stars ([Tayar et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24383)). NGBoost-XP gives ages for 15.2 million stars from astroNN labels. It scatters by 1.5–1.8 Gyr against other catalogues and is systematically younger because of the astroNN zero point ([Hou et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.17932)). SIDRA-XP gives ages for 2.2 million giants on the BINGO/APOKASC-2 scale and is restricted to RGB and high-mass red-clump stars ([Almannaei et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09040)).

XP does encode mass and evolutionary state. Networks recover Δν, νmax and ΔΠ1 for more than 2.5 million giants with accuracy "comparable to" LAMOST-based results ([Barman et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17045)), and an XP foundation model reaches σ(age) ≈ 1.33 Gyr for evolved giants ([Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03732)). Yet the SHBoost authors declined to publish XP ages for 217 million stars because age is "not directly encoded" in XP ([Khalatyan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06963)). Abundance-based calibrations put LEGACY dwarfs about 0.7 Gyr younger than asteroseismology does ([A&A 2021](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202039212)).

The link between the giant scale and the dwarf/subgiant scale is the weakest part of the system. Against Gaia parallaxes, seismic radii of dwarfs and giants differ by 4% ± 0.6% relative to each other ([Zinn et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00719)). M67 was the first cluster studied seismically all the way from subgiants to core-helium-burning giants ([Reyes et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18714)). No paper retrieved fits dwarf, subgiant and giant samples jointly to measure the offset between regimes. APOKASC-3's 9.1 Gyr thick-disc median and Xiang & Rix's 13 Gyr thick-disc onset are different statistics, so the two cannot be compared directly. In the project audit, the only direct seismic–isochrone bridge for subgiants is Chaplin et al. 2014 against Xiang, at **+0.065 dex over 110 stars** ([project audit](../docs/design-20261122-age-consensus.md)).

## Independent clocks break shared physics but number only in the hundreds

### White-dwarf cooling: the only absolute anchor for K and M dwarfs

White-dwarf cooling physics does not depend on main-sequence or giant models, and Gaia DR3 holds about 359,000 high-probability white dwarfs ([Gaia white-dwarf review](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14960)). A total age adds the progenitor's lifetime through an initial–final mass relation, and that reintroduces model dependence. Tests on 423 high-fidelity double-white-dwarf pairs show that **25% total-age errors are appropriate only for white dwarfs above 0.63 M☉ and cooler than 12,000 K** ([Heintz et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00025)). Three groups must be excluded. Q-branch objects: 5–9% of white dwarfs at 1.08–1.23 M☉ carry an extra cooling delay of ≳8 Gyr. Ultramassive white dwarfs: distillation can stall their cooling. Mergers: possibly 20% of massive white dwarfs, and they come out too young.

The El-Badry et al. catalogue holds about 16,000 white-dwarf + main-sequence wide pairs ([review](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14960)). After the mass and Teff cuts and a requirement for spectroscopy of the companion, plausibly a few hundred to two thousand would remain. That figure is an estimate, not a published count. These pairs supply the only absolute age anchor for K and M dwarfs.

### Cluster anchors: lithium boundaries, eclipsing binaries and globular clusters

LDB ages are the most robust cluster anchors. They have been re-derived homogeneously with Gaia for 9 open clusters and 3 moving groups ([Galindo-Guil et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06354)). At the old end, NGC 6791 now sits at **8.46 ± 0.66 Gyr**. That age combines its Gaia colour–magnitude diagram with eclipsing binaries and marginalises over convection, opacities, diffusion and composition ([Dufresne, Chaboyer & Rampalli 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21682)). For globular clusters, M92 is dated to 13.80 ± 0.75 Gyr ([Ying et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.02180)), and eight clusters span 11.5–13.5 Gyr with 0.5–0.75 Gyr errors dominated by distance and reddening ([Ying et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02969)). The homogeneous turn-off cluster catalogues are not anchors. All use PARSEC with fixed or constrained metallicity, and they disagree at σ ≈ 0.20–0.26 dex, with Hunt & Reffert 2023 (HR23) running about 0.1 dex young on average ([analysis of 5,056 clusters, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23802)). The audit shows the failure at the old end: HR23 dates NGC 6791 at 3.8 Gyr, while its seismic giants give 8.2–9.0 Gyr, consistent with the eclipsing-binary age ([project audit](../docs/design-20261122-age-consensus.md)).

### Clocks that test but cannot fix the zero point

Several other clocks are useful checks, but none can set the zero point:

- **Actinide chronometry.** Th/U ages are free of stellar models but carry ±2–5 Gyr per star. About 30% of stars with actinide detections are "actinide-boost" stars, for which Th/Eu fails ([R-Process Alliance 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12892); [Schatz et al. 2002](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1086/342939)).
- **The age–velocity dispersion relation (AVR).** It differs between low- and high-α discs ([Mackereth et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/489/1/176/5552689)) and steepens near 9 Gyr across R = 5–13 kpc ([A&A 2023](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2023/10/aa46666-23/aa46666-23.html)). Declining birth dispersions shape it as well as heating ([older simulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.03668)). A 10% stretch of the age scale is degenerate with the heating-law exponent.
- **[Y/Mg].** It reaches 0.45 Gyr accuracy for solar twins ([Shejeelammal et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07283)), but an NLTE study concludes it is not universal ([A&A 2026](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202660152)).
- **Co-natal equality.** Equal ages within a cluster or binary are the only fully model-free age information. Gaia EDR3 offers about **1.2 million high-confidence wide binaries** ([El-Badry, Rix & Heintz 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.05282)), including 877,416 pairs of two main-sequence stars ([2026 study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18603)).

| Clock | Independent of | Still depends on | Precision | Range | Sample | Role in the consensus |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Co-natal equality (clusters, wide binaries) | all physics | membership, unresolved companions, mass transfer | relative only | all ages | ~1.2M pairs; thousands of clusters | constraint and precision test |
| Actinide chronometry (Th/U) | stellar models | r-process production ratios | ±2–5 Gyr | >10 Gyr | handful | upper-age bound check |
| WD cooling (WD+MS, M_WD 0.63–1.0 M☉, <12,000 K) | MS/giant models | cooling models, IFMR, progenitor lifetime | ~25% | 1–10 Gyr | ~16k parent pairs; ~10²–10³ usable | K/M dwarf anchor; arbiter between gauges |
| LDB | mostly model-insensitive | pre-MS Li burning, bolometric luminosity | 7–11% systematic | 20–200 Myr | 9 open clusters + 3 moving groups | young-end fixed points |

The calibrators cover complementary age ranges. Lithium and LDB own the young end, below about 0.3 Gyr. Turn-off clusters, high-mass white dwarfs and [C/N] cover 0.3–3 Gyr. White-dwarf binaries, NGC 6791 and [Y/Mg] span the old disc. Globular clusters, the white-dwarf luminosity function (10.9–12 Gyr) and Th/U bound the halo from above. For K and M dwarfs at any age, only white-dwarf + main-sequence pairs supply an absolute anchor. No published study combines white-dwarf binaries, LDB, eclipsing-binary clusters, wide binaries and AVR tests into one validation suite for a machine-learned age catalogue. The Cool Stars 23 splinter concluded that no single indicator is reliable without cross-calibration ([Open Clusters as Laboratories, 2026](https://pith.science/paper/2609.09026)).

## Build the consensus on co-natal equality and lineage-aware maps

### Precedents and the gauge choice

No published work merges several Gaia-scale age catalogues with explicit per-catalogue offset and scale terms and calibrated coverage. Two precedents come closest. Survey of Surveys DR3 builds an internal reference from APOGEE, GALAH and LAMOST and then learns corrections for nine surveys; it cut GALAH/LAMOST Teff offsets against APOGEE from a median **44.5 K to 6.2 K** ([SoS DR3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.07936)). CHRONOS was motivated by dating techniques that disagree "up to a factor of 2", and aims at a self-consistent absolute scale ([CHRONOS I](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.25957)). The C0 measurement model transplants the Survey of Surveys recipe to ages. It has a latent log-age per star, per-source maps tiered by overlap, Student-t noise with learned inflation and floors, shared nuisance terms for correlated sources, censoring at caps, and latent ages for clusters and binaries. The evidence above supports each piece. Because training sets matter more than architectures ([Tayar et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24383)), the leverage lies in the labels, which is exactly where the consensus operates.

APOKASC-3 is the right primary gauge for reasons beyond its precision. Its scale rests on Gaia radii, and its NGC 6791 mass matches the eclipsing-binary mass. Its weaknesses are equally specific: red-clump mass loss, luminous α-rich giants, metal-poor Teff scales, and ±0.9 Gyr systematic on its own thick-disc age. Xiang & Rix is the right second gauge because its quoted errors are the only field-star errors that have passed a wide-binary test. That test rests on just 11 pairs, though, and Y² physics sits inside a 1–2 Gyr grid spread. The literature predicts a difference between the two solutions of **about 10% at solar metallicity and 2–9 Gyr**, where M67 and NGC 6791 agree. For metal-poor and old populations the difference should be larger, and of uncertain sign ([Tayar & Joyce 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09582)). The published scale term should therefore be a function of evolutionary class, [Fe/H] and age, not a single number. Two inexpensive inputs turn the two-point estimate into a measured spread: NEST emulates five grids on the same 1.3 million stars ([Boin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09540)), and kiauhoku computes per-star grid spreads ([Tayar et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07957)).

Independent physics should then choose the default gauge. The tests are NGC 6791 at 8.46 ± 0.66 Gyr, the LDB clusters, globular clusters, and the companions of white dwarfs above 0.63 M☉. Their residuals become a measured accuracy rather than an asserted one.

### Clusters and dwarf clocks as fitted quantities, not imported scales

Clusters should enter as free latent ages with priors, never as fixed labels. Each prior should come from the least model-dependent method available for that cluster: LDB for the Galindo-Guil set, eclipsing binaries for NGC 6791 and Hess-diagram fits for globular clusters. HR23 then becomes one more mapped source with a slope tier. M67 deserves special weight, because its seismology spans subgiants through the red clump ([Reyes et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18714)) while turn-off pipelines date its dwarfs. That single co-natal group is therefore a giant–subgiant bridge. Gyrochronology, activity and lithium calibrations are all built on the same ten-odd clusters whose ages disagree by 20–50% ([NGC 752 calibration, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10077v1)). Fitting those ages inside the consensus turns an imported scale into an estimated one.

Rotation and lithium should enter as observables with their own maps, not as catalogue ages. A period becomes y = ln P, with a map P(τ, Teff, [Fe/H]). The map is monotone in τ inside a declared window of about 3800–6200 K and 0.1–3 Gyr, for stars with RUWE < 1.4 and no known companion. Its slope may flatten where spin-down stalls or braking weakens, which reuses the information-aware slopes already designed for isochrone records whose posteriors simply return the prior. Conditioning on [Fe/H] is mandatory ([Kamai, Somers & Perets 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06604)). Lithium enters as equivalent width against Teff, with GALAH upper limits as censored terms, and its map has slope only inside the EAGLES Teff windows and [Fe/H] range ([Jeffries et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12197)). Activity indices (R′HK, L_X/L_bol, NUV/J) enter last, as weak terms with 0.2–0.5 dex scatter and explicit metallicity dependence. Fitted jointly with cluster latents and wide binaries, these maps yield a data-driven rotation–lithium calibration on the consensus scale. Ingesting gyro-interp or ChronoFlow ages instead would import their cluster ages and their 20–50% bias at 1–2.7 Gyr.

### Lineage, priors and outliers

Leung 2023, Martig 2016, BINGO/SIDRA, Anders 2023, DistMass, the [C/N] calibrations and, through astroNN, NGBoost-XP all descend from the Kepler–APOGEE seismic scale. They belong in one correlated group with APOKASC and must not be counted as independent votes. Chemical clocks inherit their calibrators' scales in the same way. Where a catalogue documents its prior, that prior should be divided out before combination so it is not counted twice. Examples are StarHorse's thin-disc star-formation-history prior, its uniform 0–20 Gyr rerun, and the 20 Gyr Y² grid. Age caps should be treated as right-censoring ([Boin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09540)). The 3–4% of giants that are mass-transfer products ([Frazer et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26927)) call for Student-t tails plus a flag where [C/N] and seismic masses disagree, rather than outlier clipping.

### What the predictive heads can and cannot learn

Expected precision for the frozen-latent heads depends on regime and on which inputs a star has. For subgiants, metallicity and α set the limit. XP-quality [M/H] errors of 0.1–0.2 dex translate into 1–2 Gyr for old subgiants, which makes the parallax measurement term and the latent's [α/Fe] content essential. For giants, XP carries mass-sensitive features, but the realistic target is about 0.1–0.15 dex rather than SIDRA's in-domain 0.064 dex, because spectroscopic LAMOST giant ages pass the wide-binary test only at 25–30% ([Shariat et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08675)). Young dwarfs carry information through UV excess and, after DR4, rotation. Old dwarfs should return their cell's population distribution, with an informativeness flag. Some stars reach the head only through the UV-to-IR photometric crossmatch. For them, subgiant ages built on Nataf-style UV metallicities should be treated as 16–30% rather than the quoted 8–9%, and giants without a spectral mass proxy should be returned broad. GALEX NUV can classify such stars as young or old only after correction for extinction, metallicity and white-dwarf companions.

Chemistry is a second circularity hazard alongside kinematics. The LAMOST dwarf model draws its age signal from abundances ([Wang, Xiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03019)). Leung et al. had to remove abundance information from their latent deliberately to make their ages independent of [α/M] ([Leung et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05479)). An XP latent that encodes chemistry lets a head learn the disc's age–abundance relation and impose it on accreted and halo stars. Any downstream measurement of age–[α/Fe] trends is then partly circular.

The label selection functions are known: rotation, X-ray and lithium labels skew young, subgiants are weighted by how long stars spend in that phase, and APOKASC covers only the Kepler field. Propensity weighting is required. A simple check is that stars younger than 1 Gyr should be **3–5× rarer** than ~3 Gyr stars in a Kepler-like volume ([Bouma et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06246)).

## Validation must separate precision, accuracy and shape

A validation suite has to keep three questions apart. Co-natal tests measure precision without needing any absolute scale. Independent-physics tests measure accuracy against the chosen gauge. Population tests check shape, meaning monotone ordering, compression of old ages and out-of-distribution behaviour, but they cannot fix a zero point. Confusing these is the most common error in published validations. A catalogue can pass wide-binary tests perfectly while being 1–2 Gyr off in absolute terms, as Shariat et al. explicitly caution ([Shariat et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08675)).

| Test | Data | Measures | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wide-binary z-scores (held out) | MS+MS, evolved+dwarf and WD+MS pairs; R_chance cut | relative precision per class, G and [Fe/H] bin | std(z) ≈ 1 (Xiang & Rix reached 0.81); catches 2–3× underestimates like the photometric subgiants' |
| Held-out clusters (split by cluster ID) | HR23 members; benchmark ladder | precision; prior pull | member z ~ N(0,1); young main-sequence members broad, not "3 ± 0.1 Gyr" |
| Independent-physics scale | NGC 6791 (8.46 ± 0.66 Gyr); LDB clusters; WD+MS pairs with WDs above 0.63 M☉; globular clusters (11.5–13.8 Gyr); Th/U | absolute accuracy; arbitration between gauges | agreement within combined errors; report which gauge fits better, per regime |
| Agreement between methods inside their windows | gyro vs Li below 1 Gyr; gyro vs seismic as a function of Rossby number | validity of the dwarf maps | >90% gyro–Li agreement ([Bouma et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06246)); no −47% drift above Ro☉ |
| Oldest ages | fraction above 13.8 Gyr; residual slope above 9–10 Gyr | censoring; compression of old ages | no pile-up at caps; oldest-star peak near 13.6–13.8 Gyr, as in grids extending to 20 Gyr |
| Metal-poor and accreted holdout | [Fe/H] < −1; GSE; Helmi-stream seismic ages (11.2, 12.5 Gyr); globular clusters | out-of-distribution failure; chemistry leakage | old and correctly ordered; no imprint of the disc age–abundance relation |
| AVR shape (head B0 only, no kinematic inputs) | bins of age, [α/Fe] and guiding radius | precision at old ages | sharp steepening near 9 Gyr; flat high-α AVR ([Mackereth et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/489/1/176/5552689)) |
| Chemical-clock ordering | [Y/Mg] in solar twins; [C/N] on the lower RGB at [Fe/H] > −1 | ranking | monotone, but discounted where the head uses the same features |
| Demographics | volume-limited dwarf sample | selection bias | stars <1 Gyr 3–5× rarer than ~3 Gyr stars |
| Held-out method family | drop APOKASC-2, Willett, Xiang and others in turn; predict each through its map | transfer rather than memorisation | bias ≤ 0.03 dex per class |

Conformal calibration needs one clarification. Split-conformal intervals certify coverage of the latent consensus age on the declared gauge, not coverage of the true age. The scale term must therefore be reported alongside them, never folded in. Two strata should be added to the Mondrian partition. The first is input modality: XP with a good parallax, XP alone, or photometry alone. The second is the informativeness flag, so that prior-dominated strata do not borrow coverage from informative ones.

Co-natal groups also give coverage checks that need no age label. If per-star intervals are calibrated, normalized differences between members should fall inside the expected range at the nominal rate. That makes clusters and binaries the natural coverage test for dwarfs, where no other ground truth exists. Conformal methods have been applied to rotation periods ([Kamai et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06604)), but no published Gaia-scale age catalogue reports a coverage test. This project would be the first.

## Gaia DR4 and the 2027–2030 surveys fill the bridges, not the floor

Gaia DR4 is scheduled for **2 December 2026**, based on 66 months of data. DR5 is "not before the end of 2030" ([Gaia DR4 contents](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/dr4); [Gaia news 2026](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/news-2026)). DR4's contents relevant to ages:

| DR4 product | Rows |
|---|---|
| gaia_source | **2,000,033,276** |
| Mean XP spectra | **1,824,229,813** |
| Epoch XP spectra | 121 billion |
| RVS mean spectra | **250,022,127** |
| Epoch photometry | 121.8 billion rows |
| Rotational-modulation stars | **18,237,848** |
| Eclipsing-binary candidates | 2,392,500 |
| Astrophysical parameters from XP | 999,939,280 |
| Astrophysical parameters and chemistry from RVS | 35,825,564 |

DR4 helps the age scale in several ways:

- **Subgiant metallicities.** Mean XP from nearly twice the exposure (66 versus 34 months) should improve the [M/H] and [α/M] that limit subgiant ages.
- **Single-instrument labels.** The 35.8 million RVS chemistry labels come from one instrument, which eases cross-survey zero-point problems.
- **Binary flags.** Epoch photometry and epoch XP strengthen binary flags, which matter because unresolved binaries bias single-star ages.
- **A dwarf clock.** The 18.2 million rotators become the first non-isochrone dwarf clock at Gaia scale, though they still need the selection-function treatment described above.
- **New calibrators.** The eclipsing-binary candidates are a long-term source of dynamical-mass calibrators, especially among cluster members.
- **Astrometry.** Luminosity-based seismic masses shift by more than 20% for distant stars depending on the parallax zero point ([Willett et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06870)), so the new parallaxes matter for the seismic map variants.

DR4 FLAME should enter as a new mapped source only once its priors are documented.

The next missions fill the gaps between regimes:

- **PLATO** is planned to launch in early 2027, with first calibrated data in 2028 and a goal of about 10% ages for bright solar-like stars ([PLATO team, 2026](https://indico.ict.inaf.it/event/3326/sessions/4663/attachments/12151/25372/01_Cabrera.pdf); [eoPortal](https://www.eoportal.org/satellite-missions/plato)). It is the first mission able to put large numbers of dwarfs and subgiants on a seismic scale, which is exactly the giant–subgiant bridge the two-gauge design lacks.
- **Roman.** Its Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey should yield at least **290,000 seismic giants, 185,000 of them in the bulge** ([Weiss et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04999v3)), with launch no later than May 2027 ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/asteroseismology-using-the-galactic-bulge-time-domain-survey)). It covers the metal-rich, distant bulge that APOKASC never reached.
- **SDSS-V and 4MOST.** SDSS-V's Milky Way Mapper (DR20 in July 2026; design goal 5–6 million stars) and 4MOST supply the spectroscopic [Fe/H], [α/Fe] and [C/N] that carry labels to XP ([SDSS DR20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.26149); [SDSS MWM](https://www.sdss.org/dr18/mwm/about/); [4MOST](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.02469)).
- **Rubin** could recover hundreds of thousands of rotation periods ([Schochet 2026](https://m-schochet.github.io/files/Schochet_2026_ApJS_282_10.pdf)), most efficiently for dwarfs at r ≤ 20 younger than about 2 Gyr ([LSST Science Book](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.0201)).

None of these missions lowers the shared model floor, because PLATO dwarfs and Roman giants will still be dated with model grids. What they add is overlap, which lets the consensus measure the systematic between regimes instead of assuming it.

## Conclusion

The project's most valuable output is probably not a smaller error bar but an explicit one. The literature cannot yet say whether the seismic giant scale and the isochrone subgiant scale agree for old, metal-poor populations. APOKASC-3's 9.1 Gyr thick-disc median and the roughly 13 Gyr subgiant onset have never been put on one footing. Published as a function of evolutionary class, [Fe/H] and age, and refereed by white-dwarf companions, LDB clusters, NGC 6791 and globular clusters, the difference between the APOKASC-3 and Xiang & Rix solutions would be the first measurement of that offset rather than a footnote. For most of Gaia's two billion stars, the honest product is a calibrated, population-level predictive distribution with an informativeness flag. The scientific yield will concentrate in the evolved stars with informative ages, of order ten million (existing XP age catalogues already cover 2–15 million), and, after DR4, in young dwarfs dated by rotation.

Two hidden priors deserve the same suspicion the design already applies to kinematics. The first is the cluster ladder: gyrochronology, activity and lithium all inherit isochrone ages for about ten clusters that disagree by 20–50%, and fitting those ages as latents turns the inheritance into a measured quantity. The second is chemistry: data-driven ages draw their signal from age–abundance relations learned in the disc. A chemistry-blind diagnostic, run together with accreted and metal-poor holdouts, is needed before predicted ages are used to study the chemical evolution they may already have absorbed.
